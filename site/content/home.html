See where your packets actually went.

Route Map places each router by its operator's own hostname before it asks any IP database, rejects every placement the round-trip time makes impossible, and labels each hop with where its location came from.

A recorded trace from Manila to heise.de, origin set in Settings. The first five hops, the home network and the ISP, are shown with documentation addresses.

On October 2 a commercial traceroute visualiser drew my path from Manila to amazon.com through Harare. The hop was a Tata router between Hong Kong and San Jose, answering in 215 ms, the same as San Jose; a detour through Zimbabwe would have added well over 100 ms. A trace to heise.de was worse: every Arelion backbone hop was blank, while the hostnames spelled out hnk, sng, mei, prs, ffm. The answer was in the DNS all along, and Route Map reads it.

How it places a hop

Hostname first

Operators name routers after their sites: ffm-b16-link.ip.twelve99.net is Arelion in Frankfurt. Route Map reads names with CAIDA's Hoiho rules and a table of carrier site codes, and falls back to an IP database only when the name says nothing: DB-IP Lite on your machine, or RIPEstat online.

[[pic rule1-sources png 1286 764 crop|The hop table: hops 1 to 5 local, hops 6 to 11 placed by carrier site code in Hong Kong, Singapore, Marseille, Paris and Frankfurt on Arelion's network, AS1299 with valid RPKI; hop 12 not placed; hops 13 to 15 placed in Frankfurt by DB-IP, AS12306]]

Checked against physics

Light in fibre covers about 200 km per millisecond, so each millisecond of round trip allows about 100 km: a hop that answers in 5 ms is within about 500 km, plus 300 km of slack for an approximate origin. Any placement farther than that is rejected, whatever its source, and the reason is shown. So is a database answer that would need a detour the round trip did not pay for.

[[pic rule2-rejected png 2330 180 crop wide|The Unplaced panel: hop 12, plusline-ic-323934.ip.twelve99-cust.net, location impossible for RTT: 6,964 km from hop 11 in Frankfurt and back again by hop 13, which needs 70 ms more RTT; it rose 46 ms]]

The IP database puts hop 12 in Chicago, between two Frankfurt hops. Going there and back would add at least 70 ms; the round trip rose 46 ms, so the placement is rejected.

Nothing guessed, nothing dropped

Every hop carries its source, and a hop the database knows only to a country is drawn hollow. A hop that cannot be placed stays in the table and in the Unplaced panel, with the reason it is not on the map.

[[pic rule3-unplaced png 1354 436 crop|The Unplaced panel for a recorded trace to amazon.com: hops 9, 10, 12 and 16 to 18 did not answer, so there is nothing to locate]]

Beyond where the hops are

Beside the map: the networks your packets crossed (the AS path, from DB-IP Lite ASN on your machine) with an RPKI badge for each, the countries they went through, what RIPE's route collectors see for the destination prefix, its BGP activity over the last 48 hours, and a typical latency for the same trip from RIPE Atlas anchors. Lines follow the great circle and warm with the round-trip time each step added; switch to the globe to see the path as the packets flew it. Trace again and the app compares the two runs.

No submarine cables or exchange points yet: the cable data is sold under licence, and PeeringDB's terms do not allow bundling its list. The FAQ has the details.

On your machine, with your tools

The trace runs on your computer with its own tracert, traceroute or mtr, and the status bar says which, with which flags, and which lookups answered. Hops appear on the map and in the table as the trace runs; click a marker to find its rows, or a row to find its marker. Paste a trace someone else ran, say where it came from, and it is mapped the same way. Export a PNG map, the full trace as JSON, or a PDF report with every hop, its source and the raw tool output, from the window or the command line. The same engine powers the Route Map tab in OSINTPH's FalconEye.

The source labels

hoiho
A CAIDA Hoiho rule for that operator matched the hostname and names the city.
site-code
The hostname carries a carrier's own site code, from the bundled table.
ip-db
An IP database placed it, DB-IP Lite on your machine or RIPEstat online (the label says which): a hint, since backbone addresses often sit where the prefix was registered.
ip-db, country only
Only the country is known; hollow marker, kept out of the map's fit.
local
Your network or your ISP's carrier NAT; never looked up anywhere.

No account, no telemetry, no automatic update check. One switch, Online lookups, turns every lookup off. Private, CGNAT and reserved addresses are never looked up. The full list: privacy.
