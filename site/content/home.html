Desktop traceroute map · Windows, macOS, Linux · {{tag}}

See where your packets actually went.

Route Map is a free, open source visual traceroute for Windows, macOS and Linux. It runs the trace on your own computer and draws every hop on a world map: each router is placed by its operator's own hostname before any IP database is asked, every placement the round-trip time makes impossible is rejected, and each hop says where its location came from.

How it places a hop

Most of what a visual traceroute shows depends on where it puts each router, and that is where most of them go wrong.

Hostname first

Operators name routers after their sites: ffm-bb2-link.ip.twelve99.net is Arelion in Frankfurt, and sto, hnk, sng and prs are Stockholm, Hong Kong, Singapore and Paris. For backbone routers an IP database often knows only the country, or the place where the address block was registered. Route Map reads the names first, with CAIDA's Hoiho rules and a table of carrier site codes, and falls back to an IP database only when the name says nothing: DB-IP Lite on your machine, or RIPEstat online.

[[pic rule1-sources png 1510 984 crop|The hop table: hops 1 to 3 local and 4 and 5 the ISP; hops 6 to 11 placed by carrier site code in Hong Kong, Singapore, Paris and Frankfurt on Arelion's network, AS1299; hops 12 to 16 placed by an IP database in France and Germany, two of them to the country only, with each hop's ASN and RPKI state (RIPEstat and RIPE Atlas data of 26 Oct 2026)]]

Checked against physics

Light in fibre covers about 200 km per millisecond, so each millisecond of round trip allows about 100 km: a hop that answers in 5 ms is within about 500 km, plus 300 km of slack for an approximate origin. Any placement farther than that is rejected, whatever its source, and the reason is shown. So is a database answer that would need a detour the round trip did not pay for. A destination that answers faster than its registered place allows, as anycast networks do, is placed where the round trip past the last placed hop reaches, and labelled "served from near" that city.

[[pic rule2-rejected png 1280 716 crop|The hover card for hops 7 and 8 of a saved trace to heise.de: hop 8 answered from two routers; mei-b6-link.ip.twelve99.net in Marseille is ruled out, 10,849 km away while the fastest probe allows 6,130 km, and sng-b6-link.ip.twelve99.net in Singapore is used]]

Hop 8 answered from two routers. The name of one says Marseille, 10,849 km from Manila; its fastest probe came back in 58 ms, which allows at most 6,130 km, so that place is ruled out and the other router's Singapore is used.

Nothing guessed, nothing dropped

Every hop carries its source, and a hop the database knows only to a country is drawn hollow. A hop that cannot be placed stays in the table and in the Unplaced panel, with the reason it is not on the map.

[[pic rule3-unplaced png 1510 436 crop|The Unplaced panel for a saved trace to amazon.com: hops 9, 10 and 12 did not answer, so there is nothing to locate, and hops 16 to 18, the last of the trace, never replied]]

Beyond where the hops are

Beside the map: the networks your packets crossed (the AS path, from DB-IP Lite ASN on your machine) with an RPKI badge for each, the countries they went through, what RIPE's route collectors see for the destination prefix, its BGP activity over the last 48 hours, and a typical latency for the same trip from RIPE Atlas anchors. Lines follow the great circle and warm with the round-trip time each step added; switch to the globe to see the path as the packets flew it. Trace again and the app compares the two runs.

No submarine cables or exchange points yet: the cable data is sold under licence, and PeeringDB's terms do not allow bundling its list. The FAQ has the details.

On your machine, with your tools

The trace runs on your computer with its own tracert, traceroute or mtr, and the status bar says which, with which flags, and which lookups answered. Hops appear on the map and in the table as the trace runs; click a marker to find its rows, or a row to find its marker. Paste a trace someone else ran, say where it came from, and it is mapped the same way. Export a PNG map, the full trace as JSON, or a PDF report with every hop, its source and the raw tool output, from the window or the command line. The same engine powers the Route Map tab in OSINTPH's FalconEye.

Watch keeps tracing, mtr style: every hop once a second, with loss, latency and jitter per hop, a ping plot for the hop you select and the destination, and a note whenever the path changes. Loss that only a router's own replies show is marked as rate limiting, a computer that sleeps leaves a gap, not loss, and a change of its clock is not a gap. It never probes faster than mtr's defaults and stops by itself after an hour; export the session as PDF or JSON and compare it like any trace. How it works.

Paths finds the paths a load balancer spreads your traffic over; some may stay hidden. It uses Paris traceroute and the multipath detection algorithm's stopping rule, and gives each path its own latency and loss: on the map one colour per path, in the table a row per router where they part ways. It stops after 5 silent hops, as scamper and RIPE Atlas do, then asks the target once more at full TTL on each path found. On Windows it probes over TCP; a hop that answers no TCP probe gets one ICMP echo, and the heading says whether the routers there do not answer TCP or the target answers ICMP while its TCP service stays silent. At most 20 probes a second and 1,500 in all. Reverse trace asks a RIPE Atlas probe near the target to trace back to you, with your own Atlas key, and shows both directions side by side, aligned by network. It publishes your public IP address as the measurement's target, so it asks first. Traces, Paths and Watch run over IPv4 or IPv6. Paths, reverse trace.

DNS timing. Every trace times the lookup of its target, A and AAAA, through your system resolver and any resolvers you add, and shows it with the trace: the address traced, each answer's time, whether the resolver says it is authoritative, and its TTL. Route Map resolves the name once and traces that address, so the address shown is the one traced. Plain DNS is not encrypted: only the resolvers you list are asked, and with Online lookups off only your system resolver. DNS timing.

Ports tries one TCP connection to each port you choose on the traced address, 443, 80, 22, 25, 53 and 3389 by default and at most 32, and reports each as open with its connect time, closed or filtered, with the TLS handshake time for 443. It is a reachability check for one target, not a port scanner: no ranges, four at a time. Next to Paths, an open port 443 says the target is up and the routers in between are what is silent. Port tests.

[[pic probes png 1510 754 crop|The DNS and Ports sections above the hop table for a saved trace to heise.de: the system resolver's A and AAAA answers with their times and TTLs, and ports 443 and 80 open with their connect times, 443 with its TLS handshake time, the others filtered]]

A remote agent is a server you already reach with ssh. Route Map runs one command there for each trace over your own ssh client, with no daemon on the server and no keys kept by the app, and maps the result from the server: Trace, Paths or Ports from a data centre or another country. The server needs routemap-engine for the user you log in as. Its first hops are the provider's own routers, so they are hidden by default. Remote agents.

The source labels

hoiho
A CAIDA Hoiho rule for that operator matched the hostname and names the city.
site-code
The hostname carries a carrier's own site code, from the bundled table.
ip-db
An IP database placed it, DB-IP Lite on your machine or RIPEstat online (the label says which): a hint, since backbone addresses often sit where the prefix was registered.
ip-db, country only
Only the country is known; hollow marker, kept out of the map's fit.
anycast
The destination answered faster than its registered place allows, so it is served from near you: placed where the round trip past the last placed hop reaches. An inference.
inferred from neighbours
No source placed it, but the hops either side are in one city; hollow marker. An inference.
local
Your network or your ISP's carrier NAT; never looked up anywhere.
