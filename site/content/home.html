Desktop traceroute map · Windows, macOS, Linux · {{tag}}

See where your packets actually went.

Route Map is a free, open source visual traceroute for Windows, macOS and Linux. It runs the trace on your own computer and draws every hop on a world map: each router is placed by its operator's own hostname before any IP database is asked, every placement the round-trip time makes impossible is rejected, and each hop says where its location came from.

How it places a hop

Hostname first

Operators name routers after their sites: ffm-bb2-link.ip.twelve99.net is Arelion in Frankfurt, and sto, hnk, sng and prs are Stockholm, Hong Kong, Singapore and Paris. For backbone routers an IP database often knows only the country, or the place where the address block was registered. Route Map reads the names first, with CAIDA's Hoiho rules and a table of carrier site codes, and falls back to an IP database only when the name says nothing: DB-IP Lite on your machine, or RIPEstat online.

[[pic rule1-sources png 1286 764 crop|The hop table: hops 1 to 3 local and 4 to 6 the ISP; hops 7 to 9 placed by carrier site code in Stockholm and Frankfurt on Arelion's network, AS1299 with valid RPKI; hop 10 not placed; hops 11 to 15 placed by an IP database in France and Germany, AS1299 and AS12306]]

Checked against physics

Light in fibre covers about 200 km per millisecond, so each millisecond of round trip allows about 100 km: a hop that answers in 5 ms is within about 500 km, plus 300 km of slack for an approximate origin. Any placement farther than that is rejected, whatever its source, and the reason is shown. So is a database answer that would need a detour the round trip did not pay for.

[[pic rule2-rejected png 2330 180 crop wide|The Unplaced panel for a live trace to www.spiegel.de: hop 9, link11-ic-369430.ip.twelve99-cust.net, location impossible for RTT: 10,744 km away but the round trip allows at most 3,054 km; hop 11 rejected the same way]]

The IP database puts hop 9 in France, 10,744 km from Manila. It answered in 27 ms, which allows at most 3,054 km, so the placement is rejected.

Nothing guessed, nothing dropped

Every hop carries its source, and a hop the database knows only to a country is drawn hollow. A hop that cannot be placed stays in the table and in the Unplaced panel, with the reason it is not on the map.

[[pic rule3-unplaced png 1354 436 crop|The Unplaced panel for a live trace to amazon.com: hop 6 and hops 12 to 30 did not answer, so there is nothing to locate]]

Beyond where the hops are

Beside the map: the networks your packets crossed (the AS path, from DB-IP Lite ASN on your machine) with an RPKI badge for each, the countries they went through, what RIPE's route collectors see for the destination prefix, its BGP activity over the last 48 hours, and a typical latency for the same trip from RIPE Atlas anchors. Lines follow the great circle and warm with the round-trip time each step added; switch to the globe to see the path as the packets flew it. Trace again and the app compares the two runs.

No submarine cables or exchange points yet: the cable data is sold under licence, and PeeringDB's terms do not allow bundling its list. The FAQ has the details.

On your machine, with your tools

The trace runs on your computer with its own tracert, traceroute or mtr, and the status bar says which, with which flags, and which lookups answered. Hops appear on the map and in the table as the trace runs; click a marker to find its rows, or a row to find its marker. Paste a trace someone else ran, say where it came from, and it is mapped the same way. Export a PNG map, the full trace as JSON, or a PDF report with every hop, its source and the raw tool output, from the window or the command line. The same engine powers the Route Map tab in OSINTPH's FalconEye.

Watch keeps tracing, mtr style: every hop once a second, with loss, latency and jitter per hop, a ping plot for the hop you select and the destination, and a note whenever the path changes. Loss that only a router's own replies show is marked as rate limiting, and a computer that sleeps leaves a gap, not loss. It never probes faster than mtr's defaults and stops by itself after an hour; export the session as PDF or JSON and compare it like any trace. How it works.

Paths finds every route a load balancer can send your packets along, with Paris traceroute and the multipath detection algorithm's stopping rule, and gives each path its own latency and loss: on the map one colour per path, in the table a row per router where they part ways. At most 20 probes a second and 1,500 in all. Reverse trace asks a RIPE Atlas probe near the target to trace back to you, with your own Atlas key, and shows both directions side by side, aligned by network. It publishes your public IP address as the measurement's target, so it asks first. Traces, Paths and Watch run over IPv4 or IPv6. Paths, reverse trace.

The source labels

hoiho
A CAIDA Hoiho rule for that operator matched the hostname and names the city.
site-code
The hostname carries a carrier's own site code, from the bundled table.
ip-db
An IP database placed it, DB-IP Lite on your machine or RIPEstat online (the label says which): a hint, since backbone addresses often sit where the prefix was registered.
ip-db, country only
Only the country is known; hollow marker, kept out of the map's fit.
local
Your network or your ISP's carrier NAT; never looked up anywhere.

No account, no telemetry, no automatic update check. One switch, Online lookups, turns every lookup off. Private, CGNAT and reserved addresses are never looked up. The full list: privacy.
